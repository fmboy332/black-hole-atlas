Black hole meets pulsar

A pulsar is a spinning neutron star, so this is a black hole + neutron star merger.

Simplified model of a kind of event that has been detected. This is an illustration, not real footage and not a numerical-relativity simulation.

What is real and what is theorised

  • Confirmed: LIGO and Virgo detected the gravitational waves of two black hole + neutron star mergers in January 2020, GW200105 and GW200115 (announced 2021). The black holes were roughly 9 and 6 suns, the neutron stars roughly 1.9 and 1.5 suns. No light was seen from either.
  • Not seen yet: no merger involving a visible radio pulsar has been observed. No pulsar orbiting a black hole has been found either, though astronomers are searching. Such a pair is theorised to be a superb laboratory for testing gravity. The pulsar beams here are illustrative.
  • Theorised: if the black hole is light or spins fast, the star may be torn apart, forming a disc, flinging out matter and maybe making a short gamma-ray burst and a kilonova glow. This has not been observed for a black hole + neutron star pair.

Sound is off until you press the button. The gravitational-wave chirp is sped up and shifted into hearing range where needed. Playing it restarts the animation so the inspiral lines up with the sound.

How the numbers are worked out

Chirp mass Mc = (m₁m₂)^⅗/(m₁+m₂)^⅕. ISCO from the Bardeen formula (prograde orbit). Tidal disruption radius ≈ RNS(3MBH/MNS)^⅓ with RNS = 12 km, a rough Newtonian rule. If it is smaller than the ISCO, the star is swallowed whole. Gravitational-wave frequency = 2 × the Kepler orbital frequency. Time to merger from the Peters formula for circular orbits. The final mass is a rough estimate, and the final spin is left out because no simple honest formula fits here. The on-screen orbit is slowed down enormously: the real last orbits take milliseconds.
