What is a black hole?

A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape once it crosses the boundary called the event horizon.

It is not a hole in the usual sense. It is a huge amount of mass packed into a very small space. Outside the horizon, a black hole's gravity works like any other object of the same mass. If the Sun were swapped for a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting as before. It would just be very dark and cold.

Key facts

  • Black holes are a prediction of Einstein's general relativity (1915). Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact solution in 1916.
  • The horizon radius of a non-spinning black hole is r = 2GM/c², about 2.95 km for each solar mass.
  • Black holes are confirmed by observation: gravitational waves detected by LIGO (first in 2015), and images of the shadows of the black holes in galaxy M87 (2019) and in our Milky Way (Sagittarius A*, 2022) by the Event Horizon Telescope.
  • We cannot see a black hole directly. We see its effects: orbiting stars, hot glowing gas, and ripples in spacetime.

How black holes form

Stellar-mass black holes

A star much heavier than the Sun burns through its fuel. Its core can no longer hold up against gravity and collapses. If the leftover core is heavy enough (roughly above 2 to 3 solar masses, the exact limit is uncertain), nothing stops the collapse and a black hole forms. The outer layers are often blown off in a supernova.

Merging

Black holes can also grow by swallowing gas and by merging with other black holes. Mergers produce gravitational waves.

Supermassive black holes

Most large galaxies, including ours, have a black hole of millions to billions of solar masses at the center. Sagittarius A* is about 4 million solar masses. How these grew so large so early in the universe is an open research question. Leading ideas are rapid gas feeding and repeated mergers.

Primordial black holes (hypothetical)

Some theories propose tiny black holes formed in the early universe. None has been observed.

The event horizon

The event horizon is the point of no return. Inside it, every path leads inward, so escape would need faster-than-light travel.

What it is and is not

  • It is not a physical surface. A falling observer would not feel a wall.
  • Seen from far away, a falling object appears to slow down and fade near the horizon. For the falling object itself, it crosses in finite time.

Size

For a non-spinning black hole, r = 2GM/c² (the Schwarzschild radius). For a spinning black hole with spin a (0 to 1), the outer horizon is r = (GM/c²)(1 + sqrt(1 - a²)). Faster spin makes the horizon smaller.

Nearby features

  • Photon sphere: about 1.5 times the horizon radius for a non-spinning black hole. Light can orbit there unstably.
  • ISCO (innermost stable circular orbit): 3 times the horizon radius (6GM/c²) for a non-spinning black hole. Matter closer than this spirals in.
  • Accretion disk: hot, glowing gas orbiting outside. It is the bright part we image.

Tidal forces

Stretching across a body of length L at distance r is about 2GML / r³. At the horizon this scales as 1/M², so a huge black hole is gentler there than a small one. Near a stellar-mass black hole you are torn apart before reaching the horizon. Near a supermassive one you could cross it unharmed and be stretched later, deeper inside.

Types of black holes

Type Mass Notes
Stellar-mass about 3 to 100 solar masses From collapsed stars. Many known in our galaxy.
Intermediate-mass about 100 to 100,000 solar masses Rare, evidence is still limited.
Supermassive millions to billions of solar masses In galaxy centers.
Primordial any, hypothetical Not observed.

By properties

Black holes are described by only three numbers: mass, spin, and electric charge (the "no-hair" idea). Real astrophysical black holes are expected to have almost no charge, so mass and spin are what matter. That is why the simulator uses these.

  • Schwarzschild: no spin.
  • Kerr: spinning. Spin drags spacetime around it (frame dragging).

Black hole myths

  1. "Black holes suck everything in." No. From a distance they act like any object of the same mass. Only things that get close fall in.
  2. "They are holes." They are extremely dense objects with a boundary, not an empty tunnel.
  3. "The Sun will become one." No. The Sun is not massive enough. It will end as a white dwarf.
  4. "You can see one directly." We see the glowing gas and the dark shadow, not the black hole itself.
  5. "Nothing is known about the inside." True in part. General relativity predicts a singularity, but it likely breaks down there. A full theory of quantum gravity is still missing. This is unsolved science.
  6. "Black holes last forever." Stephen Hawking predicted they slowly lose energy (Hawking radiation). For real black holes it is far too weak to see, and it has not been observed.
